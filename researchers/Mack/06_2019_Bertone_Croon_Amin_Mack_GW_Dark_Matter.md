## Abstract

The detection of gravitational waves by LIGO and Virgo has opened an unprecedented window on the universe. Beyond direct detection of massive compact objects, gravitational wave observations can probe the nature of dark matter through multiple channels: modified propagation of gravitational waves through dark matter, generation of gravitational waves by dark matter interactions or phase transitions, gravitational wave signals from dark matter-inspired compact object mergers, and more. This white paper examines how present and upcoming gravitational wave observatories offer unprecedented opportunities for unraveling the nature of dark matter across multiple candidate types and signal categories. The authors identify critical obstacles and unresolved questions requiring coordinated research bridging gravitational wave science and dark matter physics, including microphysics of dark matter-gravitational wave interactions, parameter space coverage across detection methods, astrophysical modeling, and theoretical predictions from dark matter candidates spanning ultralight bosons to primordial black holes.

## Historical Context

Gravitational waves, predicted by Einstein's general relativity a century ago, were directly detected for the first time by LIGO in 2015 (GW150914). The detection of merging black holes and neutron stars through gravitational wave observations has inaugurated a new observational window on the universe. Simultaneously, dark matter remains one of the deepest unsolved problems in physics. Despite decades of direct and indirect detection experiments, the particle nature of dark matter remains unknown.

The confluence of these two fields--gravitational wave astronomy and dark matter physics--has opened new possibilities. Dark matter, comprising ~85% of matter in the universe, necessarily interacts with spacetime and any propagating fields, including gravitational waves. Moreover, if dark matter is not fundamental but composite, or if it undergoes phase transitions, it can source gravitational waves. Primordial black holes, which might constitute dark matter or a fraction thereof, are themselves gravitational wave sources. Ultra-light bosonic dark matter (ultralight axions, fuzzy dark matter) exhibits wave-like behavior and can modify gravitational wave propagation through its coupling to spacetime curvature.

This white paper represents a broad collaboration of gravitational wave physicists and dark matter theorists recognizing that gravitational wave astronomy provides novel and complementary probes of dark matter across a landscape of candidate types.

## Key Arguments and Derivations

### Gravitational Wave Propagation Through Dark Matter

When a gravitational wave propagates through a medium (dark matter), several effects can occur. Most straightforwardly, if dark matter absorbs gravitational wave energy, the amplitude decays:

$$A(z) = A_0 \exp\left( -\int_0^z \frac{\alpha(\tilde{z})}{c} \frac{d\tilde{z}'}{H(\tilde{z}')} \right)$$

where $\alpha(z)$ is the absorption coefficient, related to the scattering cross section of dark matter with gravitational waves.

More subtle effects arise from:

1. **Birefringence**: Anisotropic dark matter or fields coupled to gravity can cause gravitational waves to exhibit different propagation speeds for different polarizations, analogous to optical birefringence.

2. **Dispersion**: Massive dark matter or ultralight bosons can impart mass to gravitational waves, altering their dispersion relation:

$$E^2 = (pc)^2 + (m_\phi c^2)^2$$

where $m_\phi$ is an effective mass acquired through coupling to the dark matter field.

3. **Lensing**: Dark matter concentrations (clumps, halos) act as lenses for gravitational waves, magnifying or demagnifying sources and creating multiple images.

### Gravitational Waves from Dark Matter Interactions

Dark matter particles or extended objects can directly generate gravitational waves through several mechanisms:

**Phase transitions**: If dark matter undergoes a phase transition (e.g., from unbound particles to bound states, or from disordered to ordered configuration), the transition can trigger gravitational wave emission:

$$\frac{d\rho_{GW}}{d\ln f} = \frac{\alpha}{12\pi} \frac{G^2 H_* v_b^3}{\Delta h^3} S(f/f_*)$$

where $\alpha$ is the transition strength, $H_*$ is the Hubble rate at transition, $v_b$ is the bubble velocity, $\Delta h$ is the barrier height, and $S(f)$ is a shape function.

**Bubble collisions**: If dark matter transitions involve bubble nucleation and collision (as in first-order phase transitions), the collision of expanding bubbles generates gravitational wave bursts.

**Dark matter-star interactions**: Dark matter captures or scattering off neutron stars or black holes generate gravitational waves. For a dark matter particle scattered from a star:

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{2 G^2 m_\chi^2 m_* v}{3 r^3}$$

where $m_\chi$ is the dark matter mass, $m_*$ is the star mass, and $v$ is relative velocity.

### Primordial Black Holes as Dark Matter

If dark matter consists entirely or partially of primordial black holes (PBHs), they naturally generate gravitational waves through mergers:

$$dN_{merge}/dt = C(z) \rho_{PBH}^2 / m_{PBH}^{5/3}$$

where $C(z)$ is a merger cross section that scales with mass and redshift. The merger rate depends sensitively on the PBH mass spectrum and clustering properties. The gravitational wave strain from a PBH merger at distance $d$ is:

$$h \sim \frac{G}{c^2} \frac{m_c^{5/3} \omega^{2/3}}{d}$$

where $m_c$ is the chirp mass and $\omega$ is the gravitational wave frequency.

### Ultra-light Bosonic Dark Matter

Ultra-light bosons (masses $10^{-22}$ to $10^{-10}$ eV) exhibit macroscopic quantum properties at astrophysical scales. When confined in a gravitational potential (star or black hole), they can form bound state clouds:

$$E_n = m_\phi c^2 - \frac{m_\phi \alpha^2 c^2}{2n^2}$$

where $\alpha \sim G m_\phi M$ is an effective fine-structure constant. Transitions between levels emit gravitational waves:

$$E_{GW} = (E_n - E_{n'}) = m_\phi c^2 \left( \frac{\alpha^2}{2} \left( \frac{1}{n'^2} - \frac{1}{n^2} \right) \right)$$

### Detection Strategies

Multiple gravitational wave detectors sensitive to different frequency ranges can probe different dark matter scenarios:

1. **Ground-based detectors** (LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA; 10-10,000 Hz): Probe primordial black holes with masses 1-100 solar masses, dark matter interactions around stellar-mass black holes, dark matter-induced merger rates

2. **Space-based detectors** (LISA; 10^{-5}-1 Hz): Probe supermassive black hole mergers through dark matter halos, intermediate-mass black hole mergers with dark matter, ultra-light boson dynamics around supermassive black holes

3. **Pulsar timing arrays** (NANOGrav, IPTA; 10^{-9}-10^{-6} Hz): Probe ultra-light bosons, dark matter mediated gravitational wave backgrounds

4. **Future detectors** (Einstein Telescope, Cosmic Explorer, AEDGE): Extended sensitivity and frequency coverage for comprehensive dark matter search

## Key Results and Opportunities

1. **PBH constraints from merger rates**: LIGO observations place constraints on primordial black hole abundance. The non-observation of the expected number of mergers rules out PBHs comprising 100% of dark matter in the mass range ~1-100 solar masses with 90% confidence, constraining the PBH population to <5% dark matter in some mass ranges.

2. **Ultra-light boson signatures**: Cloud formation around black holes from LIGO's observed sources (GW150914, etc.) predicts gravitational wave emission from transitions between Bohr levels. These spectral lines would appear as periodic modulations in strain data, with frequency determined by the bosonic mass. Current searches constrain the coupling strength and mass range.

3. **Lensing of gravitational wave sources**: If dark matter halos lens gravitational wave sources, multiple images and frequency-dependent magnifications would be observable for nearby galaxies. The lack of magnified images from distant mergers constrains dark matter clumpiness and halo mass functions.

4. **Modified dispersion from ultralight bosons**: If gravitational waves acquire effective mass from ultra-light dark matter coupling, different frequency components arrive at different times (dispersion). For a propagation distance D and effective gravitational wave mass $m_g$:

$$\Delta t \sim \frac{m_g^2 c^4}{2 E^2} \frac{D}{c}$$

For $m_g \sim 10^{-22}$ eV and cosmic distances, delays of microseconds to seconds are possible, detectable with multi-messenger observations.

5. **Phase transition gravitational wave backgrounds**: If dark matter undergoes a phase transition at high redshift (e.g., z>10), a stochastic gravitational wave background is produced. LISA and pulsar timing arrays can detect or constrain such backgrounds, ruling out or confirming dark matter phase transition scenarios.

6. **Dark matter density around merging compact objects**: The local dark matter density near a merger affects the merger dynamics and gravitational wave signature. Measurements of gravitational wave properties can back-deduce the dark matter density profile around neutron stars and black holes, providing a map of dark matter distribution in galaxies.

## Impact and Legacy

This white paper articulates a comprehensive research agenda bridging gravitational wave astronomy and dark matter physics. It has motivated numerous follow-up studies examining specific dark matter candidates and gravitational wave signatures, searches for ultralight boson signatures in LIGO data, and theoretical models of dark matter properties testable by gravitational wave observations.

The recognition that gravitational waves offer a new window on dark matter has accelerated the field's evolution toward multi-messenger astrophysics and coordinated searches across the full electromagnetic spectrum, gravitational waves, and beyond. The paper has become a foundational reference for connecting gravitational wave science to fundamental physics.
