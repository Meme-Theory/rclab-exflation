# Dark Energy and the Accelerating Universe

**Author(s):** Joshua Frieman, Michael Turner, Dragan Huterer
**Year:** 2008
**Journal/ArXiv:** arXiv:0803.0982, Annual Review of Astronomy and Astrophysics 46:385-432

---

## Abstract

The discovery a decade prior to this review that the expansion of the universe is accelerating placed the final major building block of the present-day cosmological model. This acceleration is driven by an unknown substance termed dark energy, comprising approximately 76% of the universe's energy density. The nature of dark energy remains one of cosmology's most profound mysteries. This comprehensive review examines observational evidence for cosmic acceleration, the properties and theoretical frameworks for dark energy, and prospects for future measurements. The authors identify two primary theoretical directions: dark energy could arise from vacuum quantum energy (or similar exotic form of energy with negative pressure), or cosmic acceleration may indicate that general relativity requires modification at cosmological scales. With current data favoring a cosmological constant (w=-1), the review explores cosmological constraints, the role of dark matter (20% of universe) and baryonic matter (4%), and upcoming observational tests including supernovae, baryon acoustic oscillations, weak lensing, and cluster abundance studies.

---

## Historical Context

The cosmological model as understood in the mid-20th century incorporated Einstein's general relativity with an assumption of a static or slowly evolving universe. Observations of cosmic expansion by Hubble revealed the universe was not static. However, the dominant paradigm--the matter-dominated universe decelerating over cosmic time due to gravity--persisted until 1998.

In 1998, two independent supernova teams (the Supernova Cosmology Project and the High-Z Supernova Search) announced a startling discovery: Type Ia supernovae at high redshift were fainter than expected in a decelerating universe. The data pointed instead to cosmic acceleration. This finding, recognized with the 2011 Nobel Prize, overturned three decades of cosmological expectations and demanded explanation.

Acceleration requires a form of energy with negative pressure (violating the strong energy condition), fundamentally different from matter or radiation. General relativity's field equations relate the expansion acceleration to the density and pressure of the cosmic fluid:

$$\ddot{a}/a = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}(\rho + 3p/c^2)$$

For acceleration ($\ddot{a} > 0$), one needs $\rho + 3p/c^2 < 0$, implying $p < -\rho c^2/3$. This "exotic" equation of state had never been observed in nature.

Frieman, Turner, and Huterer's review synthesizes the observational evidence, theoretical proposals, and implications of cosmic acceleration a decade into the dark energy era.

## Key Arguments and Derivations

### Observational Evidence for Acceleration

**Type Ia Supernovae**: Standardizable candles measuring luminosity distance:

$$m = M + 5 \log_{10}(d_L) + 25$$

where the distance modulus encodes the expansion history. For a universe with deceleration parameter $q_0 = \Omega_m/2 - \Omega_\Lambda$ (in ΛCDM), the expansion history is:

$$H(z) = H_0 \sqrt{\Omega_m(1+z)^3 + \Omega_\Lambda}$$

The luminosity distance to redshift z is:

$$d_L(z) = (1+z) \int_0^z \frac{dz'}{H(z')}$$

Supernova magnitudes versus redshift display the signature of acceleration: sources are fainter than expected if $\Omega_m = 1$ and $\Omega_\Lambda = 0$ (matter-dominated), but consistent with $\Omega_m \sim 0.3$ and $\Omega_\Lambda \sim 0.7$.

**Cosmic Microwave Background**: The first acoustic peak's angular position constraints the spatial geometry (flat, open, or closed):

$$\theta_A = r_s(z_{rec}) / d_A(z_{rec})$$

where $r_s$ is the sound horizon at recombination and $d_A$ is the angular diameter distance. WMAP and subsequent observations confirm spatial flatness, implying $\Omega_{total} = 1$. Combined with direct measurements $\Omega_m \sim 0.3$, this leaves $\Omega_\Lambda \sim 0.7$ to account for the missing energy density.

**Baryon Acoustic Oscillations**: The characteristic scale of acoustic oscillations imprinted in galaxy distributions serves as a standard ruler:

$$r_s = \int_0^{z_{rec}} \frac{c_s(z')}{H(z')} dz' \approx 147 \text{ Mpc}$$

Measurements of the BAO scale in galaxy surveys (SDSS, 2dFGRS, etc.) confirm the acoustic scale and thus validate the early-universe physics and expansion history consistent with acceleration.

### Dark Energy Properties

The equation of state of dark energy is parameterized as:

$$w(z) = p / \rho c^2$$

For a cosmological constant, $w = -1$ (vacuum energy density is constant). For dynamical dark energy, $w$ varies with redshift or time. Current observations strongly favor $w \approx -1$, consistent with a cosmological constant.

The density parameter for dark energy evolves as:

$$\Omega_\Lambda(z) = \Omega_\Lambda^0 / E^2(z)$$

where $E(z) = H(z)/H_0$ is the normalized Hubble parameter.

**Acceleration Mechanism**: For the acceleration parameter,

$$\ddot{a}/a = H_0^2 [1 + 3w \Omega_\Lambda / 2 - \Omega_m/2]$$

With $w = -1$ and $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.7$, $\Omega_m = 0.3$, the acceleration is positive and consistent with observations.

### Theoretical Frameworks

**Vacuum Energy (Cosmological Constant)**: The simplest dark energy candidate is quantum vacuum energy:

$$\rho_{vac} = \sum_i \hbar \omega_i / 2$$

where the sum is over all quantum field modes. The vacuum energy is expected to be huge from particle physics estimates (~10^{113} J/m^3), yet observations require $\rho_\Lambda \sim 10^{-9}$ J/m^3 (the "cosmological constant problem" or "fine-tuning problem").

**Scalar Field Dark Energy (Quintessence)**: An alternative invokes a new scalar field $\phi$ with potential $V(\phi)$:

$$\rho = \frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^2 + V(\phi), \quad p = \frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^2 - V(\phi)$$

Depending on potential shape, the field can produce varying $w(z)$ or track the background evolution.

**Modified Gravity**: Rather than new energy, cosmic acceleration could indicate gravity's modification at large scales. Examples include:

$$R + f(R) = 8\pi G T$$

where the Ricci scalar R receives modification beyond general relativity. Such theories can mimic dark energy at late times while preserving other observational successes (CMB, BBN).

### Observational Tests

**Geometric Tests**: Independent of physics, expansion history can be constrained from distance measurements:
- Supernovae to z~2.5
- Baryon acoustic oscillations
- Weak gravitational lensing
- Cluster number evolution
- Hubble parameter measurements

**Growth Tests**: Dark energy affects matter clustering growth differently than gravity:

$$\frac{d^2\delta}{da^2} + 2\frac{\dot{H}}{a}\frac{d\delta}{da} = \frac{3\Omega_m H_0^2}{2a^3} \delta$$

Modified gravity alters the growth factor $D(z) = \delta(z)/\delta(z=0)$ relative to expansion history alone. Comparing growth (from weak lensing, cluster abundance evolution) against expansion history tests modified gravity.

## Key Results

1. **Dark energy dominance**: The present-day universe is dominated by dark energy ($\Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.73$), not matter ($\Omega_m \approx 0.27$). This represents a fundamental shift in cosmological understanding, with the universe evolving toward ever-accelerating expansion as dark energy becomes increasingly dominant.

2. **Consistent cosmological constant**: Measurements from multiple independent probes (supernovae, CMB, BAO, clusters, large-scale structure) converge on a cosmological constant with $w = -1.0 \pm 0.1$, with no evidence for dynamical dark energy ($w \neq -1$).

3. **Spatial flatness confirmed**: Combining CMB spatial curvature measurements with matter density estimates confirms $\Omega_{total} = 1.0 \pm 0.02$, requiring dark energy to comprise the missing energy density.

4. **Accelerating expansion**: The acceleration parameter $\ddot{a}/a > 0$ is measured to high confidence through multiple geometric methods, with significance >3 sigma.

5. **Theoretical challenge**: Neither particle physics (predicting enormous vacuum energy) nor gravity modification provides a fully satisfactory explanation. The cosmological constant problem--why observed dark energy density is ~120 orders of magnitude smaller than theoretical expectations--remains unresolved.

6. **Future prospects**: The review identifies next-generation surveys (Dark Energy Survey, Planck, LSST, Euclid) as essential to improving dark energy constraints through larger samples and reduced systematic uncertainties, particularly geometric tests (BAO, weak lensing) and growth tests (cluster evolution, redshift-space distortions).

## Impact and Legacy

This review has become a foundational reference for dark energy research. It articulates the observational case for cosmic acceleration clearly and comprehensively, summarizes theoretical proposals, and identifies key open questions and future directions. The paper has guided subsequent research on modified gravity theories, dynamical dark energy models, and systematic uncertainty reduction in dark energy measurements.

The work emphasizes that cosmic acceleration is now established fact, and the focus has shifted toward understanding the mechanism and properties of dark energy through precision measurements.
