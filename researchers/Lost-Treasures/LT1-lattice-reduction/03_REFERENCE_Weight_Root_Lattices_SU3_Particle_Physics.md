## Abstract

This reference document synthesizes foundational material on the weight lattices and root systems of SU(3), the gauge group underlying QCD and the strong nuclear interaction. Weight and root lattices are discrete geometric structures embedded in the dual of the Cartan subalgebra. Understanding their geometry is essential for classifying irreducible representations of SU(3), assigning quantum numbers to hadrons (quarks and gluons), and determining selection rules for particle interactions. The document develops the mathematical framework and then applies it to the phonon-exflation program: finding the shortest vectors on the SU(3) weight lattice that respect observed mass ratios.

## Historical Context

The representation theory of SU(3) emerged from two parallel developments:

1. **Abstract mathematics (1950s-1970s):** Cartan, Dynkin, Weyl, and others developed the complete classification of finite-dimensional representations of compact Lie groups using weight systems and root diagrams.

2. **Particle physics (1960s-1970s):** Gell-Mann and Ne'eman independently discovered the "eightfold way" (SU(3) symmetry of the strong interaction), classifying hadrons by their SU(3) quantum numbers. This was a major step toward the quark model and QCD.

The convergence of these two streams produced modern particle physics: the Standard Model treats SU(3) as the gauge symmetry of QCD, with quarks in the fundamental representation and gluons in the adjoint.

In the phonon-exflation framework, SU(3) emerges as an internal symmetry of the compactified geometry M4 x SU(3). The framework predicts that particle masses arise from spectral eigenvalues of the Dirac operator on this space. The weight lattice structure constrains which mass eigenvalues are allowed—a key constraint for the epsilon = 0.046 gap question.

## Mathematical Structure

### Cartan Subalgebra and Roots

For SU(3), the Cartan subalgebra is 2-dimensional (rank 2). We can choose a basis {H_1, H_2} of diagonal generators. All root vectors (non-zero weights of the adjoint representation) lie in the dual space, H*.

The SU(3) root system is the A_2 root system, consisting of 6 roots:

**Simple roots:**
$$\alpha_1 = (1, 0), \quad \alpha_2 = (-1/2, \sqrt{3}/2)$$

(in an orthonormal coordinate system)

**All roots:** ±alpha_1, ±alpha_2, ±(alpha_1 + alpha_2), i.e., the six vertices of a regular hexagon in the dual Cartan subalgebra.

The root lattice Q is the integer span:

$$Q = \mathbb{Z} \alpha_1 \oplus \mathbb{Z} \alpha_2$$

This is the lattice of weights in the adjoint representation (8 roots + 1 singlet).

### Weight Lattice and Fundamental Weights

The weight lattice P contains Q and is generated by the fundamental weights:

$$\lambda_1 = \frac{2}{\alpha_1^2} \alpha_1 = \alpha_1 = (1, 0)$$

$$\lambda_2 = \frac{2}{\alpha_2^2} \alpha_2 = \alpha_2 = (-1/2, \sqrt{3}/2)$$

Wait—for SU(3) in the standard metric, the fundamental weights are:

$$\lambda_1 = (2/3, 1/\sqrt{3}), \quad \lambda_2 = (-1/3, 1/\sqrt{3})$$

These satisfy the orthogonality relation:

$$\langle \lambda_i, \alpha_j \rangle = 2 \delta_{ij}$$

The weight lattice is:

$$P = \mathbb{Z} \lambda_1 \oplus \mathbb{Z} \lambda_2$$

Since the fundamental weights are linearly independent of the simple roots (in general Q subset P), every weight in P can be uniquely written as an integer combination of fundamental weights.

### Weyl Chamber and Dominant Weights

The positive Weyl chamber is:

$$C^+ = \{ \mu \in \mathfrak{h}^* : \langle \mu, \alpha_i \rangle > 0 \text{ for all simple roots } \alpha_i \}$$

A weight mu is dominant if mu is in the closure of C^+, i.e., $\langle \mu, \alpha_i \rangle \ge 0$ for all simple roots.

**Key theorem (Weyl):** Every finite-dimensional irreducible representation of SU(3) is uniquely determined by its highest weight, which is a dominant integral weight (element of P ∩ C^+).

The finite-dimensional irreducibles can be labeled by pairs of non-negative integers (p, q) representing the coefficients:

$$\text{highest weight} = p \lambda_1 + q \lambda_2$$

### Weight Multiplicities and Character

For an irreducible representation with highest weight mu, the character is:

$$\chi_\mu = \sum_{\nu \in W \cdot \mu} \text{mult}(\nu) e^\nu$$

where W is the Weyl group (order 6 for A_2), and mult(nu) is the multiplicity of weight nu.

For SU(3), the multiplicity formula is:

$$\text{mult}(\mu) = \#\{ \nu \in W \cdot \mu' : \nu = \mu \}$$

where mu' = mu + rho and rho is half the sum of positive roots.

The character can be computed via the Weyl formula:

$$\chi_\mu = \frac{\sum_{w \in W} \text{sgn}(w) e^{w(\mu + \rho)}}{\sum_{w \in W} \text{sgn}(w) e^{w(\rho)}}$$

## Key Representations and Particle Content

### Fundamental Representation (3-dimensional)

The fundamental representation has highest weight lambda_1 = (2/3, 1/sqrt(3)). Its three weights form a triangle in weight space:

$$\Lambda = \{\lambda_1, \lambda_1 - \alpha_1, \lambda_1 - \alpha_1 - \alpha_2\} = \{\lambda_1, \lambda_1 - \alpha_1, -\lambda_2\}$$

In physics, these three weights correspond to the three colors of quarks: up, down, strange (or red, green, blue in modern language). The physical interpretation is:

- Weight lambda_1: quark with hypercharge Y = 1/3, isospin I_3 = +1/2
- Weight lambda_1 - alpha_1: quark with Y = 1/3, I_3 = -1/2
- Weight -lambda_2: quark with Y = -2/3 (strangeness)

### Adjoint Representation (8-dimensional)

The adjoint representation has highest weight alpha_1 + alpha_2 (the highest root). Its 8 weights are the 6 roots plus the zero weight (with multiplicity 2, corresponding to the 2 Cartan generators).

In particle physics, the 8 weights of the adjoint representation are the 8 gluons (color-anticolor pairs), plus 1 singlet. The physical gluons are:

- 3 positive roots: r_anti-b, b_anti-g, g_anti-r
- 3 negative roots: b_anti-r, g_anti-b, r_anti-g
- 2 zero weights: isosinglet gluons (mixing of r_anti-r, g_anti-g, b_anti-b)

### Decuplet and Octet Baryons

Baryons are composite states of three quarks, transforming under SU(3) ⊗ SU(3) ⊗ SU(3) ≈ 27 + 10 + 10* + 8. The most important are:

- **Octet (8):** nucleons (p, n), hyperons (Lambda, Sigma, Xi). Highest weight = lambda_1 + lambda_2.
- **Decuplet (10):** Delta, Sigma*, Xi*, Omega. Highest weight = 2(lambda_1 + lambda_2).

Meson (quark-antiquark) representations are given by 3 ⊗ 3* = 8 + 1, yielding an octet (pions, kaons, eta) and a singlet (eta').

## Weight Lattice Geometry and the Closest Vector Problem

### Metric on Weight Space

The weight lattice P embeds in the Cartan subalgebra dual h* with the standard Euclidean metric derived from the Killing form:

$$\langle H, H' \rangle = \text{Tr}(\text{ad}_H \text{ad}_{H'})$$

For SU(3), normalized so that the long roots have length squared 2:

$$\langle \alpha_i, \alpha_i \rangle = 2, \quad \langle \alpha_1, \alpha_2 \rangle = -1$$

This induces a metric on the weight lattice:

$$d(\lambda_i, \lambda_j)^2 = \sum_{a,b} G_{ab} (\lambda_i - \lambda_j)_a (\lambda_i - \lambda_j)_b$$

where G is the matrix of inner products.

### Closest Vector Problem (CVP) on SU(3) Weight Lattice

**Problem statement:** Given a target vector **v** in h* (representing observed particle mass ratios or other observables), find the weight lattice point **w** ∈ P that minimizes $|\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{w}|$.

**Current context in phonon-exflation:**

The framework posits that particle masses arise from the spectrum of the Dirac operator $\mathcal{D}$ on M4 x SU(3). The eigenvalues should align with weight lattice structure. However, quantum corrections introduce an epsilon ≈ 0.046 gap between predicted and observed mass ratios.

The CVP reformulation asks: Is there a weight lattice vector close enough to Lambda_obs to explain the epsilon gap via lattice structure alone, without invoking new physics?

### Applying LLL to the Weight Lattice

To apply the LLL algorithm to CVP on the SU(3) weight lattice:

1. **Represent the lattice basis:** Use fundamental weights lambda_1, lambda_2 as basis vectors:
   $$B = \begin{pmatrix} 2/3 & -1/3 \\ 1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{3} \end{pmatrix}$$

2. **Embed the target:** Represent Lambda_obs in h*:
   $$\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2$$

3. **Run LLL reduction:** Compute the reduced basis of P.

4. **Search for closest vector:** Use a reduced basis search algorithm (Kannan, Fincke-Pohst) to find the closest weight lattice point to **v**.

5. **Measure the gap:** Compare the distance from **v** to the closest lattice point against epsilon.

## Key Results and Observations

1. **Rank = 2:** The SU(3) weight lattice is 2-dimensional. This is a small dimension, making LLL reduction and CVP search computationally tractable (polynomial in dimension).

2. **Lattice determinant:** The fundamental domain of P has area (determinant):
   $$\det(P) = |\det(B)| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 0.577$$

3. **Hexagonal structure:** The weight diagram of the fundamental representation is a regular triangle in the weight lattice, reflecting the 3-fold rotational symmetry. The root lattice Q has a hexagonal structure (6-fold symmetry of the root system).

4. **Sublattice index:** The quotient P/Q has order 3 (for SU(3)), meaning P has three cosets of Q. This is relevant for distinguishing quark flavors (up, down, strange correspond to three Q-cosets).

5. **Metric degeneracies:** The weight space metric has inherent symmetries (Weyl group orbits). Shortest vectors may form orbits under the Weyl group, leading to degeneracies in CVP solutions.

The weight lattice structure is fundamental to the phonon-exflation mechanism for several reasons:

The Dirac operator $\mathcal{D}$ on M4 x SU(3) has eigenvalues constrained by:

$$\text{spec}(\mathcal{D}) = \{ \text{numbers related to } P, Q, \text{ and root structure} \}$$

The Standard Model particle masses are predicted to arise from these eigenvalues. If the eigenvalues form a discrete set on the SU(3) weight lattice (or its cosets), then observed masses must lie near lattice points—the CVP problem.

### 2. The Epsilon = 0.046 Gap

Current computations predict mass ratios that differ from observation by epsilon ≈ 0.046 (or ~4.6%). This gap is precisely the kind of discrepancy that CVP on the weight lattice can address:

- If Lambda_obs is NOT on the lattice P, the closest lattice vector is at distance epsilon.
- If epsilon can be explained by the geometry of P (i.e., the closest lattice point is uniquely determined and inherently distant from Lambda_obs due to lattice structure), then no new physics is needed.
- If epsilon is anomalously small or large compared to expected lattice geometry, new physics or computation errors are flagged.

These transitions correspond to lattice defects (domain walls, vortices) in the order parameter. The weight lattice structure determines which symmetry-broken phases are accessible.
