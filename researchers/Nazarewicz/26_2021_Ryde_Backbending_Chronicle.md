# Chronicle of the Discovery of the Back-Bending Phenomenon in Atomic Nuclei: A Personal Recollection 50 Years On

**Author(s):** Hans Ryde
**Year:** 2021
**Journal:** European Physical Journal H 46, 23
**arXiv:** 2109.08144
**Relevance:** MEDIUM

---

## Abstract

A chronicle describing the historical context and the development of ideas and experiments leading to the discovery of the back-bending phenomenon in rapidly rotating atomic nuclei some 50 years ago is presented. The moment of inertia of some atomic nuclei increases anomalously at a certain rotational frequency, revealing important clues to our understanding of nuclear structure. I highlight the decisive interactions and contacts between experimentalists and theorists, which created the right environment, allowing for the revelation of an undetected phenomenon in Nature. Finally, I reflect on the key points allowing for the discovery and particularly point to the importance of systematic surveys, which in this case investigated the energy levels in heavy nuclei of a large sample of elements, as well as to the accuracy of the measurements of the ground state levels made at the time.

## Key Arguments and Derivations

### Section 1: Introduction and Motivation

Ryde describes how his systematic investigation of energy levels in heavy nuclei, inspired by Bengt Edlen's earlier atomic spectroscopy surveys, led to the serendipitous discovery of backbending. After returning from Caltech in 1965, he used 43 MeV alpha-particles from the Stockholm cyclotron to study a range of nuclei with $A \approx 100$--$200$ in $(\alpha, xn)$ reactions, analyzing the dependence of nuclear energy levels on rotational excitation energy.

### Section 2: Unexpected Deviations

Deviations were first noticed in $^{161}$Er rotational bands (1969). Ryde discussed the anomalies with Aage Bohr and Ben Mottelson in Copenhagen. In February 1969, Bohr suggested plotting the moment of inertia $\mathcal{I}$ as a function of $\omega^2$ (the cranking model representation). When applied to $^{160}$Dy data, the hand-drawn plot by Bohr and Mottelson in their October 1970 letter showed the first "up-turn" -- the first representation of backbending. Arne Johnson was responsible for the $^{160}$Dy data analysis; Josef Sztarkier developed the decisive coincidence unit for $\gamma$-ray identification.

Subsequent papers presented $^{158}$Dy, $^{160}$Dy, $^{162}$Er, and $^{168}$Yb, with $^{162}$Er showing the archetypal S-curve of backbending.

### Section 3: Early Interpretations

Two interpretations were advanced:
1. **Coriolis effects** (Stephens and Simon, 1971): theoretical treatment of Coriolis interaction on nucleons at high angular momentum
2. **Pairing collapse** (Bohr and Mottelson): at high angular momenta, rotational energy exceeds pairing energy, breaking Cooper pairs. This is a phase transition from superfluid to independent-particle motion, increasing the moment of inertia. The concept was introduced by Mottelson and Valatin (1960) as an analogy between rotation breaking nuclear pairs and magnetic field breaking electron pairs in a superconductor.

Zdzislaw Szymanski and Ray Sorensen, visiting Stockholm in 1970-1971, provided crucial theoretical support. The working names for the phenomenon included "S-curve," "Superband," "Stockholm band," and Szymanski's "bow of a viking ship" before "back-bending" became standard.

### Section 4: Detector Technology

The replacement of NaI(Tl) scintillation detectors with Li-drifted germanium Ge(Li) crystals was essential. Ryde acquired early test crystals from a company in Santa Monica while at Caltech (1963-65). The higher energy resolution enabled the precision measurements of ground-state rotational bands necessary for observing backbending. The subsequent development of multi-detector systems (NORDBALL, EUROBALL) followed from the need to measure angular distributions of $\gamma$-rays.

### Section 5: Reflections

Key points enabling the discovery:
1. Systematic survey of many elements (tedious but rewarding)
2. Accuracy of ground-state level measurements (enabled by new Ge detectors)
3. Decisive theorist-experimentalist interactions (Bohr-Mottelson, Szymanski, Sorensen)

Ryde notes the analogy with neutron star glitches -- abrupt increases in rotational velocity due to internal structural changes -- as occurring on vastly different scales but involving similar physics.

## Key Results

1. Backbending = anomalous increase in moment of inertia at critical rotational frequency
2. First observed in $^{160}$Dy (1970), archetypal S-curve in $^{162}$Er
3. Physical mechanism: rotational alignment breaks Cooper pairs (pairing collapse)
4. Analogous to magnetic-field-induced pair breaking in superconductors (Mottelson-Valatin, 1960)
5. Coriolis interaction on high-$j$ nucleons provides microscopic mechanism
6. Standard representation: $\mathcal{I}$ vs $\omega^2$ plot (suggested by Bohr, 1969)
7. Discovery enabled by: systematic surveys + Ge(Li) detector precision + theorist collaboration
8. Recent work: backbending frequency increases for neutron-deficient nuclei ($^{88}$Ru, Cederwall et al. 2020)
9. Neutron star glitches as macroscopic analog of nuclear backbending
